# What it takes to build a neural-network bot for Troll Farm — an analysis for the owner

Written 2026-08-29 by `local_claude_1` on the owner's word ("analyse what should be done to create a
bot with neuronetwork"). Facts come from four reads of this repository made today (the sources are
listed at the end); the estimates are mine and are marked as such. Nothing here touches the platform.

## The answer in short

1. **It can be done, and the proof is on the ladder.** The #1 player, delineate (30.89 on the ladder,
   33.77 in the contest), plays with a neural network alone — no search, no lookahead. About 101
   thousand numbers ("parameters", the weights the training sets), 98 thousand characters of source
   out of the 100 thousand allowed, 2–3 ms of the 50 ms a turn. Its author published the recipe — what
   the network sees, how it is built, how commands come out of it, the five training stages — and we
   hold that text verbatim.
2. **We already hold most of the pieces.** A game engine in Rust that matches the official referee
   exactly and runs a turn in 0.3 microseconds; a training environment with exactly delineate's
   observation shape, built here in July; a proven way to ship a trained network inside one Rust
   file (a 35-thousand-parameter network ran at 7 ms a turn in a 69 KB file); 24,973 recorded ladder
   games with every command of every player, and an exact turn-by-turn reconstruction of the board
   validated on 784 games of the top four.
3. **What is missing:** the full-game environment (July's stopped at a fixed opening with the training
   decision made by a rule), the head of the network that chooses which troll to train, the
   turn-by-turn production of several trolls' commands, a "board → move" dataset for imitation, the
   training curriculum, the compute run — and the software runtime itself: PyTorch (the training
   library) is not installed, Python is 3.10 where the project needs 3.11, the disk is 97 % full.
4. **Recommendation: a bounded programme in five gated phases** — (0) the runtime back, (1) the
   full-game environment on real maps, (2) a clone of the top players' moves as the starting policy,
   (3) PPO training from that start with a clone anchor, against the champion and against itself,
   (4) export, bed, one ladder hour. **The first cheap probe** (about a week, the bots chartered):
   can a cloned policy play a whole game in our bench without collapsing, and what does it score
   against the champion? That one number tells us whether the road is open before any training
   compute is spent.
5. **Odds and price.** Five to eight weeks to a first ladder reading. My estimate: about one chance
   in three that the first neural candidate beats the champion of record (18.2 today, 21.2 in
   yesterday's field), about one in seven that it reaches the top-four band (27 and above) within
   two months; the repository's own July estimate for a first programme was one in five to one in
   ten. The price you accept up front: a network's play cannot be read as rules. "Control over the
   code" becomes control over the recipe, the data and the gates — the way delineate's author had it.

## 1. What "a bot with a neural network" means here — and the proof

**Words first.** A *neural network* is a large table of numbers (weights) arranged in layers; you put
in a description of the board and it puts out a score for every possible move; the move with the
best score is played. *Training* adjusts the weights so those scores lead to wins. *PPO* (proximal
policy optimization) is the standard way to train by playing: the network plays many games, is
rewarded for what worked, and its weights are nudged a little at a time so it does not forget what it
knew. *Behaviour cloning* (imitation) is the other way: show the network recorded boards and the move
a strong player made, and make it predict that move. *Self-play* means the network plays against
copies of itself. A *curriculum* is a ladder of easier tasks before the full game.

**delineate's bot, as its author described it** (`local_claude_1/reconstructions/sources/delineate-gist.github.com-2026-05-25.md`,
restated in `local_claude_1/reconstructions/delineate/ALGORITHM.md`):

- **What it sees.** 104 "planes" of numbers over the largest board (11 rows × 22 columns; smaller
  maps padded): the cell type, each tree's kind, size, health, fruits and regrowth timer, which troll
  stands where with what talents and cargo, distances to the shacks, the current training target with
  its costs and what is still missing, global counts, and a flag marking *the troll being decided*.
  The author's own caveat: "I don't think the exact observation planes are all that important and I
  would guess anything sensible would work."
- **How it is built.** A small convolutional network (a "ResNet" of four blocks — the kind of network
  that reads a grid the way image networks read pictures) producing, for every cell, 13 scores — one
  per action type: move here, harvest, plant (four tree kinds), chop, pick (four kinds), drop, mine —
  3,146 scores in all; plus a separate head of 144 scores over every possible troll to train next
  (speed 1–3 × carry 1–4 × harvest 0–2 × chop 0–3, with illegal combinations masked; one slot means
  "done training"). Two value heads (a value head estimates how good the position is; the training
  needs it, the finished bot does not).
- **How commands come out.** One pass of the network chooses the training target; it is written back
  into the input; then one pass *per troll* (only the "troll being decided" flag changes) gives that
  troll's command — a move is chosen as a destination cell among the cells it can reach; then a small
  beam search over the trolls' combinations only to keep them from colliding — never to look ahead.
  Why per troll: one pass for all trolls "seems to have trouble with some basic path finding".
- **How it was trained.** PPO throughout, five stages: (1) build one prescribed troll, rewarded for
  getting closer to the bill; (2) random targets, big reward on completion, the training order fired
  automatically; (3) random target troll counts 2–5 with the true end-of-game score difference, plus
  +0.5 for every wood deposited (and wood counted 3.5 instead of 4 at the end, so it does not hoard);
  (4) movement frozen, a separate head learns *which* troll to build on pure score difference — because
  when the network chose its own targets with a completion bonus "it would learn to build lots of cheap
  units to spam the completion bonus"; (5) everything fine-tuned on the score difference. "It was
  truly remarkable to see how much the network improved in the final 36 hours of training."
- **What failed for them.** Plain PPO on the whole game, no curriculum, reached about 25th and
  "could never learn to mine iron — the benefits to mining iron are extremely far in the future".
  Residual defects at the end: trolls circling forever or stuck mining; "I tried to manually patch up
  each of these edge cases but several still remain". Not stated anywhere: how many games, how many
  machines, how many days, the learning-rate settings.
- **The result.** #1. In the game author's statistics over 673 Legend games: 525 wins, 146 losses,
  418.5 points against 297.8, zero timeouts. In our corpus (223 games): 78.5 % wins, 415 against 253,
  93 % of its points from wood.

**Nobody else in Legend did this.** Of the twelve published Legend write-ups, eleven are hand-written
search or rules; the only other reinforcement-learning entrant reported 390th place. The #2, norxondor
(29.66), is a fast rule bot — rules can reach 30 too. This analysis is about the network route because
that is what you asked; the rule route stays what it is.

## 3. What this project tried in July, and what it taught

Between 2026-07-13 and 07-31 the project ran a large learning programme — all of it offline analysis,
**no learned bot was ever submitted**. The index is `docs/CONSTRAINTS.md` (the entries around lines
181–246, 491–530, 754–796, 1078–1081, 1153–1171) and the journal
`data/analysis/live-agent-6553250/legend-top3-experiment-cycle-2026-07-18.md`. Six lessons carry over:

1. **Train on real maps, never on generated ones.** The one near-miss — a convolutional "option
   critic" choosing at turn 75 between the resident and a farm plan — gained +36.5 points a game on
   generated maps and lost 78 on real ones: the generator always drew 6 water cells, real maps have
   12–104 (D29–D32). The real-map corpus exists now; there is no reason to repeat this.
2. **PPO from a teacher collapses unless anchored.** A network started from a rule teacher fell from
   94 % to 18 % task success under plain PPO; a small auxiliary imitation term (weight 0.10) rescued it
   to 99 % every time. This is the project's one durable positive learning result — and it is the same
   device that makes "clone first, then PPO" safe.
3. **Small networks cannot copy a rule system's integer filters** (cloning a rule teacher reached
   85 % where the rules are 100 % reproducible by a decoder; "when the teacher is a rule system, fit a
   rule system"). Cloning *delineate* — itself a network — from the board planes is a different question
   and has never been tried: the ledger names "spatial planes at decision states on the official
   substrate" as **the single untried observation class**.
4. **Fit statistics anti-predict transfer.** Across ~30 supervised selectors, lower fit error went with
   *worse* play (correlation +0.89); a "native imitation" that scored 77 % intent accuracy lost 172
   points a game in closed loop. Judge every learned thing by playing games, never by held-out
   accuracy. (Your top-down rule, in the language of learning.)
5. **A network inside the rule bot is closed.** Learned selectors on hand-made features — a dozen
   designs, ending with 80 thousand exact counterfactual labels — never cleared a +1.5 gate; the ledger
   marks the question **★ FINAL** and says reopening requires an owner decision citing that entry
   (`docs/CONSTRAINTS.md:491–500`). "Only execution-class changes (waste cuts, geometry, packaging)
   have transferred to the arena." The route this analysis proposes is not that route: it is the whole
   policy as a network over the board, which the same entry leaves open.
6. **Shipping is solved; speed is not generous.** The int8 kernel path works to the byte, but at 7 ms
   for 35 thousand parameters. A delineate-sized network (101 thousand) through our kernel would take
   ~20 ms a turn on this host, and the platform's machines are not faster. Either the kernel gets
   ~5× faster (delineate's did 101 thousand in 2–3 ms, so it is possible) or the network stays at or
   under ~50 thousand parameters. The source budget is the other cap: a pure network bot has ~85
   thousand characters for weights after the kernel code — about 68 thousand int8 parameters in base85
   text (base85: five text characters carry four bytes), or ~90 thousand at 6 bits each.

The July programme's own reason for stopping is the most important fact: the curriculum reached
97–99 % on its narrow tasks and the live kernel worked, but the actor only ever controlled **a fixed
opening with the training decision made by a rule**; "the active research boundary is autonomous
recipe/first-move selection and then strategic field transfer". It was superseded by the selector work
(lesson 5) and never resumed. delineate's stages 4–5 are exactly the missing piece — the plan head
trained on pure score difference with movement frozen, then everything fine-tuned.

## 4. Three ways to do it

**Way A — delineate's recipe as written: PPO from scratch through the five-stage curriculum, in our
engine, on real maps.** Everything is documented; our environment and network match its shapes; the
July curriculum is its stages 1–3 in miniature. Cost: the full-game environment with the plan head and
per-troll passes (a week), then the training runs (weeks of CPU). Risk: the curriculum is the art —
delineate's author spent the contest on it and still had trolls circling; without their settings we
would rediscover them. Their plain-PPO failure ("never learned to mine iron") is the trap this route
walks past only if the stages are right.

**Way B — clone first, then PPO (recommended).** Build the "board → move" dataset from the exact
reconstruction of the top four's games (784 games validated; ~150 thousand troll decisions per player,
doubled by the board's point symmetry), train the network to predict their primitive commands (the
move to the next cell, harvest, plant kind, chop, mine) and, for the plan head, the troll they actually
trained next (known in hindsight from the replay — a clean label the July readiness audit did not
consider). Then PPO from that start with the clone as the anchor (lesson 2), against the champion, the
orchard bots and past copies of itself, on real maps, with delineate's score-difference reward and wood
shaping. Why this is better than A: the start already walks, harvests, mines iron and trains — the
long-horizon lessons PPO cannot find alone are given, and the anchor keeps them; the first result (the
clone playing in the bench) comes in a week and costs no training compute. Risks: cloning primitives
has failed here on hand features (lesson 3 — but planes are untried); the top three have only ~200
games each (Bubaptik's 3,921 span 34 versions — usable with care); a clone may copy the *look* of
strong play without its judgment, and the −172 of July says a clone that plays *worse* than its
teacher's average is the normal outcome before PPO. The remedy is built in: the clone is a start, the
bench decides, PPO does the improving.

**Way C — a small network inside the rule bot (a learned target choice, a learned value for plans).**
Not recommended. It is the route the ledger closed after three weeks and ~40 designs; it inherits the
rule bot's ceiling; and it is not what the #1 did.

**Not on the list:** buying delineate's weights (not published) or search-based bots with a learned
evaluation (nobody in Legend reported one; our engine is fast enough that a search bot is a separate,
real option — but it is not a "bot with a neural network").

## 5. The programme, phase by phase (in the card style: done / dead / budget)

**Phase 0 — the runtime back (1–2 days; a bot can do it).** Python 3.11 with a CPU PyTorch installed
through `uv` in the project; one July trainer (`train_level1_ppo.py`) re-run small; its checkpoint
exported through the int8 kernel generator and played in the bench. Free the disk: ~100 GB moved to
the USB archive (needs your word — deletions are not mine to make). *Done:* a network trains, exports
and plays a game here. *Dead:* nothing; this is plumbing.

**Phase 1 — the full-game environment (4–6 days; codex_1 / claude_1 build, I review).** Extend the
level-1 backend to the whole game: both seats controllable (self-play), the 13-action grid with masks,
the 144-way train-plan head, the per-troll passes with the "troll being decided" flag, the collision
check, real maps drawn from the corpus, an opponent pool of our linked rule bots (the champion, orchard
6, the roster of 22), and a parity test: a game played in the environment and then replayed through the
reconstruction gives identical states. *Done:* 1,000 self-play games with no illegal command and
parity 1,000/1,000. *Dead:* parity cannot be reached in the budget.

**Phase 2 — the clone (5–7 days; the first real number).** The dataset from the 784 exact games (plus
Bubaptik's latest version and, as a weaker filler, our own 11,606 games if needed): every troll-turn as
(planes, command), every TRAIN as (planes, talents). Train the 35–50-thousand-parameter network to
predict them; report held-out accuracy per verb **and then ignore it** (lesson 4). The number that
counts: the clone playing 24 real maps against the champion in the bench — does it finish games, does
it train, does it mine, and what is its score. *Done:* a clone that plays 24/24 games to the end
without loops or timeouts and scores at least half the champion's points. *Dead:* after a week it
cannot play a whole game — then the planes or the labels are wrong, and Way A is the fallback.

**Phase 3 — PPO from the clone (2–4 weeks of CPU; the long phase).** The clone as the start and as
the anchor (weight ~0.1, decayed); reward = end-of-game score difference with delineate's shaping;
opponents = the champion and orchard 6 first, then a pool of past checkpoints; real maps; the four
board sizes mixed. Gate every few days by a fixed bench: 400 games against the champion, 400 against
orchard 6, on the same seeds, both seats. *Done:* ≥ 60 % wins against both with a positive mean margin,
stable over three consecutive gates. *Dead:* no gain over the clone after 2×10⁸ turn-steps, or the
policy found a hole in our engine (a sample of its games fails the replay parity check).

**Phase 4 — ship (3 days + one ladder hour).** int8 export, the move-for-move parity bed (Python
network against the Rust kernel), the 100-thousand-character check, ≤ 15 ms a turn on this host (the
platform's machines are slower; the limit is 50), a readable diff, codex_1's reproduction, your
prediction, one hour, one reading — after codex is done with the platform. *Done:* the reading is on
the ledger with its 160 games read. Then the loop you know: read the games, find the defect, fix it in
the recipe or in a thin rule patch (as delineate's author did), retrain, re-read.

## 6. Machines, storage, time, people

- **Compute (my estimates).** A 35-thousand-parameter network needs about 4.5 million multiply-adds
  per pass; with one pass for the plan and one per troll, 3–6 passes a turn. On 20 cores with batched
  inference that is roughly 2–3 thousand turns a second during play, about half that while training:
  ~40–80 million turn-steps a day. Phase 3's 2×10⁸ turn-steps is three to five days of the host; a
  delineate-sized network is 3–4× slower. Feasible on the CPU for a small network, marginal for a large
  one. A cloud GPU would be ~10× faster; last time its results did not match the CPU's, and the grant
  is nearly spent. My recommendation: the host's CPU, the small network, the VM left to the ladder queue.
- **Storage.** The corpus is 17 GB on a disk with 34 GB free; the datasets of Phase 2 add ~5–10 GB;
  checkpoints ~1 GB. Something must move to the USB archive first.
- **Time.** Five to eight weeks to the first ladder reading, with a real number after two (the clone).
- **People.** In your rule ("Fable is expensive — delegate"): codex_1 and claude_1 build Phases 0–2 on
  the VM and their tests; training runs on this host under my supervision; I design, gate, integrate
  and write to you; you read the clone's games at Phase 2 and the candidate's at Phase 4.

## 7. The risks, ranked — and what a network will not fix

1. **The clone does not walk (Phase 2 dead).** Cloning failed here twice on other representations.
   Fallback: Way A's stages from scratch, with the July levels as the base — slower, still open.
2. **PPO finds a hole in the engine rather than a strategy.** The engine is referee-exact after the
   parity programme, but a policy searches for cracks harder than any rule bot did. The replay-parity
   check on samples of its games is the guard; anything it exploits gets fixed in the engine first.
3. **Reward hacking of the plan head** — delineate's own trap (cheap trolls for the completion bonus).
   Their fix (score difference only for the plan head, movement frozen) is in the recipe.
4. **Speed on the platform.** Our kernel is 10× slower per parameter than delineate's; the 50 ms
   limit is hard and three overruns lose the game. Cap the network at ~50 thousand parameters until
   the kernel is measured on a batch of real games.
5. **The reading is one number, ±1.5, once an hour.** Training decisions must rest on the bench
   (thousands of games); the ladder confirms.
6. **Opacity.** The network gives no rule to read. What you can read are its games (as you do now),
   its recipe, its data, and the bench; delineate's author patched residual defects by hand. Your goal
   of 08-26 was "≥ 25.40 **and** control over the code / cleanliness": a network bot keeps the second
   only in the weak sense of a clean kernel plus a weight table. This is the decision that matters most.
