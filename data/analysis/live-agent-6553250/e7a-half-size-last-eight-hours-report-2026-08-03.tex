\section*{1. What game problem are we solving?}

In Troll Farm, a program controls trolls on a grid. Trolls move, collect fruit, mine iron,
chop trees, plant fruit and bring resources back to their tent. The bot must coordinate its
workers: one blocked doorway or one bad planting decision can waste many turns. Fruit has value,
but wood is especially important for the final score. A good policy therefore has to balance
short-term collection, training a second troll, denying resources to the opponent, creating a
renewable orchard and converting remaining fruit into wood before the game ends.

The live program, called \textbf{E7a} in the project, is already competitive. Its latest mature
checkpoint was rank 11. The engineering challenge is deliberately harder than ordinary code
cleanup: remove half of the source while preserving the decisions that made it competitive.
The target is exactly half of 62,820 bytes, so a qualifying source may contain at most
31,410 bytes.

This was a logical simplification exercise. Variable names were not shortened to meaningless
letters, the source was not compressed, and formatting was not crushed merely to save bytes.
Each candidate was built by deleting unused generality or replacing a large decision block with
a smaller, named policy that can still be read and reviewed.

\section*{2. How the tests work, in plain language}

Every candidate was compared with the exact live bot on the same generated maps, against the
same opponents, and from both starting sides. This paired comparison removes much of the noise:
if both programs receive the same difficult map, their difference is more informative than
either raw score alone.

\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{38mm}Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Term used in the records} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
Baseline & The exact 62,820-byte live E7a program used as the reference. \\
Candidate & A proposed smaller program being tested against the baseline. Iteration labels such as
``r5'' or ``r32'' are simply revision numbers, not new algorithms by themselves. \\
Paired margin change & For one game, first calculate ``our score minus opponent score'' for each
program, then subtract the baseline result from the candidate result. Positive is better. \\
Development panel & A reusable collection of known maps used to diagnose and improve code. Passing
it is encouraging, but it cannot prove that the program generalizes because engineers have seen
its results. \\
Untouched or fresh panel & A set of map numbers that is locked before any map is generated or
inspected. It is run once. Its result is final for that exact source, preventing repeated tuning
until a lucky test appears. \\
Opponent family & One of six fixed opponent programs with different strategies. Requiring success
across families prevents a candidate from improving only against one convenient style. \\
Bootstrap lower bound & A conservative estimate obtained by resampling whole maps 50,000 times.
It asks whether the average improvement might be an accident of the particular maps selected. \\
Catastrophe & A game lost by at least 100 points. The candidate may not create more catastrophes
than the baseline. \\
Negative-margin mass & The total size of all losses. A 200-point loss counts more than a 5-point
loss. This catches a candidate that wins often but occasionally collapses. \\
Period-2 oscillation & A troll alternates between two positions, A--B--A--B, instead of completing
its job. A run of six or more alternating moves is treated as a serious liveness defect. \\
Transfer & Evidence that a policy which looked good on development maps also works on unseen maps. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

The full panel contains 43 maps, two starting sides and six opponent programs: 516 paired games.
Other checks verify compilation, legal command generation, second-troll training, execution time,
empty-input handling and replay of known oscillation examples. A candidate must pass \emph{all}
frozen gates; a good average cannot excuse a crash or a worse catastrophic-loss tail.

\section*{3. The work in chronological order}

\subsection*{3.1 First cut: prove half-size is mechanically possible}

The initial byte audit identified large blocks that were general but unnecessary for this live
two-worker policy: support for arbitrary worker counts, unused protocol and trait machinery,
generic target containers, unused runtime state and broad fallback policies.

The first integrated cut, revision 5, was only 30,949 bytes and compiled successfully. It was a
useful mechanical proof, but a behavioral disaster: over a 16-game smoke test it lost 262.5
points more than the baseline on average and produced 14 catastrophes. Restoring economic
forecasting and coordination repaired revision 18 to only 6.6 points worse, but it had grown to
35,146 bytes---3,736 bytes over the limit. This established the central trade-off: most of the
original size was tied to behavior that really mattered.

\subsection*{3.2 A size-qualified version that removed too much}

Revision 32 was 31,387 bytes, 23 bytes under the ceiling. It compiled, passed ten fixed semantic
examples and moved acceptably in small tests. The full 516-game development panel exposed the
failure: average paired result $-53.66$, catastrophes 19 to 64, and total loss mass 4,138 to
15,143. All six opponent styles became worse. Small smoke tests had been too narrow.

Block-by-block diagnosis showed two important facts. First, deleting the orchard alone already
cost 7.64 points on average even though the remaining source was still 48,644 bytes. Second,
keeping the exact economic core but placing it under a simplified outer policy cost 27.45 points
at 33,167 bytes. Restoring the exact tuned opening recovered much of the loss, to $-9.81$, but
raised size to 36,059 bytes. The opening and orchard were therefore not disposable decoration.

\subsection*{3.3 Structural specialization instead of blunt deletion}

The next approach preserved the valuable opening and economics while specializing the program
to what the live bot actually does: two workers, a smaller set of targets, fewer zero-value
branches and less unused state. A 31,337-byte version passed the development panel with average
$+5.53$, conservative lower bound $+1.82$, catastrophes improving 19 to 11, and no long
two-cell oscillations.

Its one-shot unseen test rejected it. The average remained positive at $+3.30$, but the result
was map-fragile and total loss mass increased from 4,385 to 4,891. This was the first strong
warning that the remaining problem lived in rare tail events rather than in average play.

\subsection*{3.4 Repairing tent traffic and orchard geometry}

Replay traces found two concrete coordination failures:
\begin{itemize}
\item A faster rear troll carrying wood could pin the front carrier near the tent. The repair
gave the front carrier priority so the pair moves like an ordered convoy.
\item Starting an orchard could consume too much access space around the tent. The repair allowed
a mixed-use tent door only when a third usable door remained free.
\end{itemize}

The resulting 31,398-byte candidate passed development strongly and then passed its untouched
516-game panel: average $+5.55$, lower bound $+1.29$, fewer catastrophes and lower loss mass.
It looked ready---until exact replays of real Arena games exposed an empty-selection crash and,
after the crash was made safe, long two-cell oscillations in 10 of 25 known bad games. A program
that can freeze a troll is not safe to deploy even when aggregate scores are good, so this
candidate was superseded.

\subsection*{3.5 Stopping repeated backtracking}

The next family of candidates remembered recent positions. If a newly chosen move would recreate
an A--B--A--B pattern, the movement resolver blocks or redirects it. This made every tested
half-size candidate liveness-clean: the fresh panels went from more than 100 long oscillation
episodes in the baseline to zero.

The first guarded version was 31,405 bytes. It also deleted three pieces of logic to pay for the
new memory: speculative reservation of tent stock, part of a rule for leaving a fully funded
tent, and an occupied-door prefilter. It was excellent on development maps ($+9.45$ average,
$+4.04$ lower bound) and on unseen maps ($+9.46$, $+1.74$ lower bound). Nevertheless, the strict
tail gate failed by one: catastrophes changed from 26 to 27. Later replay attribution showed
that the dangerous deletion was the simplified rule for evacuating a fully funded tent, not the
stock-reservation deletion.

\subsection*{3.6 Preserve tent evacuation; simplify the oscillation guard}

A 31,248-byte successor restored the original tent-evacuation rule and replaced the larger
oscillation detector with a compact rule: do not immediately move back to the previously observed
cell. It passed development at $+9.14$ with fewer catastrophes and no long oscillations. On its
fresh panel it remained positive at $+3.92$ and improved the loss tail, but it failed breadth:
the average was negative against two of the six opponent styles. That source was rejected without
Arena submission.

Traces then showed why an unconditional ``never step back'' rule is too coarse. Some harmful
reversals occur at a tree, where changing direction abandons work. Other reversals happen on an
open route and are legitimate corrections. Thresholds based on worker role, worker count or
opponent workforce did not separate these cases reliably.

\subsection*{3.7 Tree-aware backtracking: the newest exact candidate}

The newest source blocks the second consecutive reversal when either end of the move is a tree,
but permits a bounded correction on an ordinary open route. This rule is called the
``tree-edge reversal'' policy in internal filenames: an \emph{edge} simply means the move between
two adjacent map cells.

The source is 31,407 bytes---three bytes below the target---and remains readable. It passed the
full development panel at $+8.22$ average, $+3.02$ lower bound, catastrophes 19 to 12, and zero
long oscillations. On the one-shot unseen panel it still averaged $+6.29$ and passed 11 of 13
gates, but catastrophes worsened 12 to 16 and total loss mass worsened 4,567 to 4,826. It was
therefore rejected and not submitted.

\section*{5. Latest diagnosis after the newest rejection}

The rejected fresh maps can still be replayed for diagnosis, but they can never qualify another
source. Five controlled variants were compared on the exact same 516 preserved rows.

\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{43mm}r r r Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Variant, explained} & \textbf{Bytes} & \textbf{Mean} & \textbf{Cats.} & \textbf{Loss mass} \\
\midrule
Strictly forbid immediate backtracking & 31,248 & +6.41 & 19 & 5,017 \\
Allow one bounded correction over five moves & 31,394 & +6.22 & 16 & 4,823 \\
Block repeated reversal at a tree edge & 31,407 & +6.29 & 16 & 4,826 \\
Over-limit control retaining more exact tent logic & 31,848 & +6.37 & 19 & 4,928 \\
Retain stock reservation, but with a liveness flaw & 31,394 & +6.53 & 17 & 4,776 \\
Remove the compact orchard completely & 28,517 & $-38.72$ & 48 & 15,719 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

Here ``Cats.'' is the number of at-least-100-point losses; the baseline had 12, and baseline
loss mass was 4,567. These diagnostic numbers show that the newest fresh failure is \emph{not}
caused solely by the tree-aware movement rule, stock reservation, or the smaller tent-door logic.
The tree-aware rule is actually better than the strict rule on the tail. They also show that
globally deleting the orchard is disastrous, despite saving almost 2.9 kilobytes beyond the
required target.

The worst fresh task was examined command by command. It used map number 9,866,014, our first
starting side, and the adaptive gold-strength opponent. The baseline won its score margin by
100; the half-size candidate lost by 259, a 359-point reversal.

Both programs issued the same commands through turn 14. On turn 15, the baseline starter moved
outward toward a natural tree. The smaller program moved back toward home. On turns 18 and 19 it
picked up and planted an apple, activating the compact orchard, while the baseline continued
working the natural tree. The candidate then spent time waiting at and harvesting the orchard
as the opponent expanded. This is the first strategically meaningful divergence in the worst
loss.

That trace does \emph{not} justify deleting the orchard. The no-orchard control lost 38.72 points
on average and tripled loss mass. The evidence instead supports a narrower next question:
\textbf{can orchard activation be made fail-closed and closer to the exact parent decision?}
In plain language, the smaller bot should start an orchard only when it can prove that doing so
will not abandon a more valuable natural-tree route or delay the economy at the wrong moment.

The joint goal is therefore \textbf{not complete}. It would be misleading to call the 31,407-byte
source ``the new best bot'': it is the newest technically valid half-size research candidate,
but it is transfer-rejected. The best current Arena bot remains the original full-size E7a.

\section*{7. Recommended next work}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Preserve the orchard but narrow its activation.} Compare the exact parent's reason
for continuing toward a natural tree with the compact policy's reason for returning home and
planting. Add the smallest readable condition that protects that choice.
\item \textbf{Keep the tree-aware liveness rule.} It removes long A--B oscillations and is better
than the global no-backtrack rule. Do not spend bytes restoring a rule that the diagnostics have
already shown to be weaker.
\item \textbf{Re-test on development and known failure traces first.} The candidate must keep
second-worker training, avoid tent blockage, and not recreate the orchard collapse.
\item \textbf{Use a new untouched range only after the source is frozen.} The last fresh range is
consumed. Reusing it can diagnose mechanisms but cannot provide honest transfer evidence.
\item \textbf{Submit only after every local gate passes.} Then recover the exact Arena source by
hash and wait for enough live games to establish rank 15 or better.
\end{enumerate}
